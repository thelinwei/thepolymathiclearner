Doubling
the Square三家倍方

A Greek geometer, a Vedic altar-builder and a Chinese calculator are each handed the same square and asked for one twice its size. All three find the line. Each gives it a different kind of answer.

διπλάσιον χωρίον द्विकरणी 倍幂
00

The resultoutcome first, the story after

The problem: here is a square; make one exactly twice its area. Today we write the answer in two symbols, √2, and move on. Three older mathematical cultures met this same square, and what they handed back tells you as much about them as about the number.

Athens · Plato's Meno, c. 385 BCE · Pythagorean arithmetic
“Build it on the diagonal.” The line is exact, and it has no number.

The diagonal is shown, then proved to be incommensurable with the side: no whole-number ratio fits it. The Greeks then built a ladder of fractions, the side-and-diameter numbers, that closes in on it from both sides, rung after rung.

Altar ground · Baudhāyana & Āpastamba Śulbasūtra, 1st millennium BCE
“Stretch the cord across.” On a twelve-finger brick: seventeen fingers, less one sesame seed.

A recipe in one breath: the measure, plus its third, plus that third's fourth, less a thirty-fourth of that fourth. The rule names itself saviśeṣa, “with a little over.” Carried onto a twelve-finger brick, it is off by less than a thousandth of a sesame seed.

Counting board · 九章算术, Han · 刘徽注, 263 CE
一尺四寸一分四厘二毫…… and the rods can keep going.

The answer is a procedure, run digit by digit on counting rods. When it never ends, Liu Hui gives two honest choices: 以面命之, name the number by its side, or 求其微数, keep extracting ever-finer decimal parts.

What the experiment found

All three agree to five decimal places, 1.41421. The Vedic rule's value is 577 / 408. That same fraction turns up independently on the eighth rung of the Greek ladder, and again as the second step of Heron of Alexandria's averaging rule. Three routes, one fraction.

Liu Hui's commentary reads almost like a review of that fraction. He describes the same kind of fractional patch the Śulba rule uses and judges it 虽粗相近，不可用也: close, but not for use, because the true value cannot be pinned by it. The Śulba authors had already flagged the same thing in a single word, saviśeṣa.

Each answer has the shape of how its makers knew things. The Greek answer is a line you can see. The Vedic answer is a cord you can make. The Chinese answer is a procedure you can keep running.

How this was run. The same problem went to each tradition, and each answers with its own surviving texts, tools and numerals. The texts are quoted in Greek, Sanskrit and classical Chinese. Where a text is silent (how Baudhāyana reached his 1/34, for instance), the reconstruction is marked as one. The imagined exchange near the end is play, and is labelled as such.

Table I · Athensa stick, some sand, and a boy who gets it wrong twice

Plato, Meno 82b–85b · c. 385 BCE · then Pythagorean arithmetic as reported by Theon of Smyrna, c. 100 CE

Socrates wants to prove a point about learning, so he calls over one of Meno's household slaves, a boy who has never studied geometry, and draws in the sand. A square, two feet on each side, so four square feet. Now, he asks: what square is twice as big, eight square feet? How long is its side?

The boy answers the way almost anyone would. Double the area, so double the side: four feet. Socrates draws it, and they count. The square has grown to sixteen, four times the original. The boy tries again, splitting the difference at three feet. That gives nine, still too big. Now he is stuck, and Socrates is pleased, because being stuck is where the learning starts. Then comes the move. Inside the four-foot square, Socrates draws a diagonal across each of the four small squares, and the four diagonals close into a tilted square.

Fig. 1 · The boy's three answerstap through

Each tilted side cuts a two-by-two square exactly in half, and four halves of four is eight. The doubled square stands on the diagonal. The boy agrees, and Socrates hands the answer its name:

Plato, Meno 85b · Greek
Καλοῦσιν δέ γε ταύτην διάμετρον οἱ σοφισταί· ὥστ᾿ εἰ ταύτῃ διάμετρος ὄνομα, ἀπὸ τῆς διαμέτρου ἄν, ὡς σὺ φῄς, ὦ παῖ Μένωνος, γίγνοιτ᾿ ἂν τὸ διπλάσιον χωρίον.
“The experts call this line the diameter. So if diameter is its name, then the double space would come, as you say, boy of Meno, from the diameter.”

Notice what Socrates never says: how long it is. He gives no number at all. The answer is a construction, a line you can point at. For Plato that is the whole point: the boy has seen, with his own eyes, a relation that was already true.

Then the floor gives way

Somewhere in the Pythagorean circle, perhaps a generation or two before Plato, someone tried to measure that diagonal against the side using a common unit, some small length that fits a whole number of times into both. There isn't one. However small the unit, it never fits both. The Greek word for this was ἀσύμμετρος, “without common measure.” The ratio itself was later called ἄλογος, which means both “without ratio” and “unspeakable.”

Aristotle passes the argument along as a textbook example of proof by contradiction. If the diagonal were commensurable with the side, odd numbers would equal even ones. In modern clothes it runs like this.

Plate G1 · the odd-even argument
d/s = p/q in lowest terms ⟹ p² = 2q² ⟹ p even, p = 2r ⟹ q² = 2r² ⟹ q even
How to read itSuppose diagonal-to-side is a fraction p/q already cancelled down. The square on the diagonal is twice the square on the side, so p² is even, which makes p even. Feed that back in and q comes out even too. A cancelled fraction with both parts even cannot exist, so there was never a fraction to begin with.

The Greeks counted anyway: the side-and-diameter ladder

Proving that no fraction is exact did not stop anyone from wanting good ones. The Pythagorean πλευρικοὶ καὶ διαμετρικοὶ ἀριθμοί, side numbers and diameter numbers, are reported by Theon of Smyrna. They work like a growing staircase. Start with a side of 1 and a diameter of 1. For the next rung, the new side is side + diameter, and the new diameter is twice the side + diameter. Each rung's diameter over side is a fraction for √2, alternately a little under and a little over, and each pair misses the perfect relation by exactly one unit.

Fig. 2 · Side and diameter numbers, in Greek numeralsclimb
RungSide · DiameterGreekDiameter ÷ sided² − 2s²
A Greek wrote numbers with letters: α = 1, ι = 10, ρ = 100. So ιζʹ is 17, and φοζʹ is 577.

Look at rung eight: 577 over 408, written φοζʹ over υηʹ. Hold on to that pair. It is about to walk in from the other side of the world.

Table II · The altar grounda cord, two pegs, and a brick that must be right

Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra and Āpastamba Śulbasūtra · late Vedic Sanskrit · roughly 800–400 BCE

The Śulbasūtras, “rules of the cord,” are construction manuals for fire altars. An altar might need to be rebuilt at twice the area, or reshaped from a square into a falcon without changing its area by a grain. The tools are a rope (rajju), pegs (śaṅku) and fired bricks of fixed sizes. The units are bodily: the aṅgula, a finger's breadth, and below it the tila, a sesame seed. Baudhāyana sets thirty-four sesame seeds, laid side by side, to one finger.

For the doubling problem the rope gives the answer at once, just as the sand did in Athens:

Āpastamba Śulbasūtra 1.5 · parallel in Baudhāyana
समचतुरश्रस्याक्ष्णयारज्जुर्द्विस्तावतीं भूमिं करोति ।
samacaturaśrasyākṣṇayārajjur dvistāvatīṃ bhūmiṃ karoti
“The cord stretched across the diagonal of an equal-sided square makes ground of twice that size.”

Up to here, Athens and the altar agree. The difference is what comes next. An altar is built from bricks, and a brick has to be cut to a length. So the sūtra goes on and gives the diagonal a measure, in the most compressed sentence of the whole experiment:

Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra (i.61–62 in Thibaut's numbering) · Āpastamba 1.6
समस्य द्विकरणी । प्रमाणं तृतीयेन वर्धयेत् तच्चतुर्थेनात्मचतुस्त्रिंशोनेन सविशेषः ॥
samasya dvikaraṇī. pramāṇaṃ tṛtīyena vardhayet tac caturthenātmacatustriṃśonena saviśeṣaḥ
“The double-maker of the square: increase the measure by its third, and that third by its own fourth less the thirty-fourth part of that fourth. This is saviśeṣa, with a little over.”

Read in the altar-builder's own units, the rule becomes a cord you can actually lay out. Take the twelve-finger brick, the prādeśa, and walk the rule along a rope:

Fig. 3 · The rule laid out on a twelve-aṅgula sidestep

That last step is the wonder. A thirty-fourth of an aṅgula is exactly one sesame seed. The whole rule, on the standard brick, comes to seventeen fingers less one sesame seed. The true diagonal is shorter than that cord by less than a thousandth of a seed's width. Georg Thibaut, who first edited Baudhāyana in the 1870s, suspected that the odd count of thirty-four seeds to the finger was chosen to fit this rule. A later commentator on Āpastamba works the same rule in fingers and seeds and lands on “forty-two aṅgulas joined with fourteen tilas,” which is the rule applied to a side of thirty aṅgulas.

Where might 1/3, 1/12 and 1/408 come from?

The sūtra gives no derivation. It is a rule to be remembered and used. A reconstruction going back to Thibaut and developed by Bibhutibhusan Datta uses only the moves the sūtras already teach elsewhere: cutting bricks into strips and wrapping them around a square as an L-shaped band (a gnomon). Step through it below. Each step is something you could do with real bricks on the ground.

Fig. 4 · Two squares into one, by strips · reconstructionstep

The final shave is the subtle one. Taking a strip of width x off two sides removes almost exactly 2·(17/12)·x of area. It also removes one tiny corner square, x², twice over, and the rule quietly ignores that. That ignored speck is why the result is a hair too long, and why the sūtra names it, without apology, saviśeṣa.

Table III · The counting boardrods, columns, and a procedure that keeps going

九章算术 Nine Chapters, compiled by the 1st c. CE · 刘徽注 Liu Hui's commentary, 263 CE

The Chinese calculator sits at a board marked into columns and lays out bamboo counting rods. Numbers are place-value from the start: units upright, tens lying flat, hundreds upright again, so neighbouring columns never blur. Zero is an empty column. The Nine Chapters are organised as problems, answers and 术, procedures. Our problem would sit in two places. First, in the right-triangle chapter:

九章算术 · 卷九 勾股
勾股术曰：勾股各自乘，并，而开方除之，即弦。
“The procedure for base and height: multiply each by itself, add them, and extract the square root. That is the hypotenuse.”

Our square has base 1 and height 1, so the doubled square stands on the 弦. Liu Hui then shows why the procedure holds, by colour and rearrangement. The square on the base is painted vermilion (朱方), the square on the height blue-green (青方), and their pieces are moved, 出入相补, out of one place and into another, until they exactly fill the square on the hypotenuse.

刘徽注 · 勾股术
勾自乘为朱方，股自乘为青方，令出入相补，各从其类，因就其余不动也，合成弦方之幂。开方除之，即弦也。
“The base times itself makes the vermilion square; the height times itself, the blue-green square. Let what goes out and what comes in make up for each other, each kind to its kind, leaving the rest unmoved, and together they form the area of the hypotenuse square. Extract its root, and that is the hypotenuse.”
Fig. 5 · 出入相补 · out and in, the pieces make up the wholetap
朱方 1 + 青方 1. Four half-squares, cut on the diagonal.

The geometry is settled. But the Nine Chapters always want the number as well, and for that the board has the 开方术, the root-extraction procedure in chapter four. It is one of the oldest written square-root algorithms anywhere:

九章算术 · 卷四 少广 · 开方术
开方术曰：置积为实。借一算，步之，超一等。议所得，以一乘所借一算为法，而以除。除已，倍法为定法。其复除，折法而下。
“Set the area as the dividend (实). Borrow one rod and step it along, skipping a column each time. Estimate the digit; multiply the borrowed rod by it to make the divisor (法), and subtract. That done, double the divisor to make the fixed divisor (定法). To subtract again, shift the divisor down a column …”

Here is our problem, posed the way the Nine Chapters pose things, and run on a simplified board:

posed in the manner of the Nine Chapters, for this experiment
今有方一尺，欲倍其幂。问方几何？
“Here is a square of one chi on a side. We want to double its area. What is the side?”

Fig. 6 · 开方 on the rods · √2, one digit per passstep

The rods never run out of work. The remainder never reaches zero, and the text has a sentence ready for that case: 若开之不尽者，为不可开，当以面命之. If the extraction does not come out even, the number “cannot be opened,” and one should name it by its side, 面. Liu Hui's commentary on that sentence is one of the finest passages in the whole tradition:

刘徽注 · 开方术
术或有以借算加定法而命分者，虽粗相近，不可用也。……其数不可得而定。故惟以面命之，为不失耳。……不以面命之，加定法如前，求其微数。微数无名者以为分子，其一退以十为母，其再退以百为母。退之弥下，其分弥细，则朱幂虽有所弃之数，不足言之也。
“Some procedures add the borrowed rod to the fixed divisor and name a fraction. Roughly close, but not for use. … Its number cannot be fixed. So only naming it by its side loses nothing. … Or, without naming it by its side, add to the fixed divisor as before and seek its fine numbers. Fine numbers without a unit name become numerators: one step down takes ten as denominator, two steps down a hundred. The further down, the finer the parts, until what is discarded from the vermilion area is not worth speaking of.”

Liu Hui offers two answers to an endless number, and both are honest. 以面命之 keeps it exact by keeping it as “the side of 2,” much as we write √2. 求其微数 runs the rods on into tenths, hundredths, thousandths of the last unit, which is a decimal expansion some thirteen centuries before Simon Stevin. In the readout above, every digit past the 毫 is written exactly by his rule: 十分毫之一，百分毫之三, “one part in ten of a hao, three parts in a hundred.”

The maths, side by sidethe same number, three engines

Put into one modern notation, the three tables are doing recognisably related things, each with its own engine underneath.

Plate G2 · Greek · a two-term recurrence
s(n+1) = s + d, d(n+1) = 2s + d, d² − 2s² = ±1
How to read itEach rung is built from the last by adding. The highlighted identity is the engine: every pair misses “diameter squared equals twice side squared” by exactly one, switching sign each time. As the numbers grow, that fixed miss of one becomes a vanishing share, so the ratio d/s homes in on √2, alternately from below and above. This is the same equation later called Pell's, x² − 2y² = ±1.
Plate V · Śulba · a correction on a correction
√2 ≈ 1 + 1/3 + 1/(3·4) − 1/(3·4·34) = 577/408
(17/12)² = 2 + 1/144 ⟹ 2·(17/12)·x ≈ 1/144 ⟹ x = 1/408
(577/408)² = 2 + 1/166464 (saviśeṣa)
How to read itThe first three terms give 17/12, whose square overshoots 2 by 1/144. The gnomon shave removes a strip of width x from two sides, area about 2·(17/12)·x. Setting that equal to the overshoot gives exactly 1/408, which is 1/(3·4·34). The neglected corner is why the square of the answer still exceeds 2 by a sliver, the saviśeṣa.
Plate C · 算學 · one digit per pass
(10a + b)² = 100a² + (20a + b)·b
√(a² + r) ≈ a + r/(2a) or a + r/(2a+1)
How to read itLine one is the whole 开方术. With root-so-far a, the next digit b must fit inside the remainder as a band of width b wrapped around the square: 20a is the doubled divisor 定法 shifted a column, and b is the newly estimated digit 议. It is the same L-shaped band as the Vedic gnomon, cut to a decimal width. Line two shows the two fractional patches Liu Hui rejects, one lying above the true value and one below. The Śulba step 17/12 → 577/408 is the first kind, a + r/(2a), applied with a negative remainder.
Plate K · the engine under two of them
x(k+1) = (x + 2/x)/2: 1 → 3/2 → 17/12 → 577/408 → 665857/470832
How to read itAverage a guess with 2 divided by that guess. Heron of Alexandria wrote this rule down in the 1st century CE, and today it is Newton's method. Run it from 3/2 and the second step is Baudhāyana's 577/408. Its iterates are exactly rungs 2, 4, 8 and 16 of the Greek ladder. The Vedic rule, the Greek ladder and Heron's averaging are one family, each moving at its own pace.

Three speeds

How fast does each table close in? The chart counts correct decimal places after each step of work. The steps are not equal labour. A rung is two additions, a Heron step is a division, a rod pass is one digit. Still, the shapes tell the story: the ladder climbs steadily, the rods add one place per pass, and the averaging rule roughly doubles its correct places each time.

If the three tables metan imagined exchange · play, built from real lines

None of these people met. Where a line is in quotation marks it is their own text; the rest is staging.

Altar

Your square of eight feet: the cord says its side is seventeen fingers less one sesame seed, if the small square is a twelve-finger brick. We laid such bricks by the thousand.

Athens

Seventeen to twelve, corrected by one part in four hundred and eight. We have that fraction too, on the eighth rung. It is beautiful, and it is not the diagonal. Nothing written with numbers is. The diagonal is ἀσύμμετρος to the side.

Altar

We never said it was. Read the last word of the rule: saviśeṣa. With a little over. The cord is long by less than a thousandth of a seed. The fire does not notice.

Board

“虽粗相近，不可用也。” Close, but not for use, as a definition. Your fraction overshoots, and its kin undershoots. “其数不可得而定。”

Athens

Then you agree with me. It has no number.

Board

It has a name, 面, the side of two, and it has as many digits as you have patience for. Put down your stick and hand me the rods. “退之弥下，其分弥细。”

Here the rigour ends and the wondering begins

Set side by side, the three tables give three verbs for what it means to have an answer.

to seeAthens · the line
to makeAltar · the cord
to proceedBoard · the rods

In Athens, knowing means seeing a relation that holds forever, so the most honest answer is a line together with a proof that no number fits it. On the altar ground, knowing means being able to build it on time and to tolerance, so the best answer is a recipe that names its own slack in a word. At the counting board, knowing means having a procedure that can always take one more step, so the answer is the procedure itself, and the number is whatever you choose to stop at.

What delights here is the overlap. A Vedic recipe, a Pythagorean ladder and Heron's averaging rule all reach 577/408. The Chinese band (20a+b)b and the Vedic gnomon are the same L-shape, cut to different widths. Liu Hui and the Śulba authors both say plainly that their number is not the last word. These are three dialects of one geometry, each saying something the other two leave unsaid.

An uninvited fourth guest makes the point. A small Old Babylonian clay tablet at Yale, YBC 7289, shows a square with its diagonals and the value 1;24,51,10 in base sixty, about 1.41421296. Earlier than all three tables, and correct to six places.
