Three Tables · experiment 02 · one problem, three traditions

Squaring
the Circle三家方圆

The same three tables, a harder problem. Here is a circle; make a square of exactly the same area. The Vedic builder needs it for an altar, the Greek wants it as a proof, the Chinese calculator wants the field measured. None of them can do it exactly, and each finds a different way to live with that.

τετραγωνισμός मण्डलं चतुरश्रं 割圆
00

The resultoutcome first, the story after

The problem: here is a circle; make a square with exactly its area. Today we know the side must be (√π/2)·d, and that since 1882 no ruler-and-compass construction can produce it. None of our three tables had that verdict. Each still had to answer, and each answered in its own way.

Altar ground · Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra I.58–60 · 1st millennium BCE
Two rules, circle to square and square to circle, that agree with each other to five places.

The round household altar and the square offering altar must have the same area. The sūtra gives a cord recipe each way, and never names a ratio like π. The two recipes are near-perfect inverses, so a circle built from a square and squared again returns almost exactly. The circle they build is about 1.7% too large.

Syracuse · Archimedes, Measurement of a Circle · c. 250 BCE
A circle equals a triangle, and its ratio lies between γ ιʹ/οαʹ and γ ζʹ.

The square itself stays out of reach. Archimedes proves the circle equal to a right triangle (radius × circumference) and then traps the ratio between two fractions using 96-sided polygons: 223/71 below, 22/7 above. The answer is a proved interval.

Counting board · 九章 圆田术 · 刘徽 割圆术, 263 CE · 祖冲之, 5th c.
割之弥细，所失弥少: cut finer, lose less, and the number arrives.

The Nine Chapters already say the circle's area is half the circumference times half the diameter. Liu Hui then cuts the circle from 6 to 192 sides with nothing but the 勾股 rule, reaching 3.14 and later 3.1416. Two centuries on, 祖冲之 pins it between 3.1415926 and 3.1415927.

What the experiment found

Archimedes and Liu Hui computed the same number. Archimedes' lower bound from the 96-gon's perimeter and Liu Hui's area of the 192-gon are one quantity, 3.14103…, reached from opposite ends of the world, 500 years apart. They had to agree: both rest on the identity circle = half circumference × radius, which the Greek text proves and the Nine Chapters state as a working rule, 半周半径相乘得积步.

The Vedic rules were tuned for agreement between altars. Their implied ratio, 3.088, misses π by 1.7%, but their two directions match each other to five decimal places. The fit that mattered was between the round earth-altar and the square heaven-altar.

And Archimedes' upper bound 22/7 is exactly 祖冲之's 约率, his "rough ratio," held next to his "close ratio" 355/113. The Greek answer is an interval you can prove. The Vedic answer is a pair of rules that keep the altars in balance. The Chinese answer is a cut you can always make finer.

How this was run. Same method as experiment 01: each tradition answers with its surviving texts, tools and numerals. The numbers in the figures are computed live from each method. Where the texts are silent, the page says so, and the closing exchange is labelled as play.

01

Table I · The altar groundearth is round, heaven is square, and both must hold the same fire

Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra I.58–60 (Thibaut numbering) · Āpastamba III · Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa VII

This time the altar ground goes first, because it is the one place where the problem was not optional. On the ritual ground an east–west line is staked out. At its western end sits the household fire, the gārhapatya, which stands for this world. At the eastern end sits the offering fire, the āhavanīya, which stands for heaven. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa explains why the western hearth is round: the womb is round, and this world is round. The eastern altar is square. Some schools allowed the household altar to be square as well, and Baudhāyana records both customs. Where it is round, it must hold exactly the area of the square.

So the cord has to work in both directions. First, from square to circle:

Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra I.58 · circling the square
चतुरश्रं मण्डलं चिकीर्षन्नक्ष्णयार्धं मध्यात्प्राचीमभ्यापातयेत् । यदतिशिष्यते तस्य सह तृतीयेन मण्डलं परिलिखेत् ॥
caturaśraṃ maṇḍalaṃ cikīrṣann akṣṇayārdhaṃ madhyāt prācīm abhyāpātayet | yad atiśiṣyate tasya saha tṛtīyena maṇḍalaṃ parilikhet
“Wishing to make a square into a circle, swing half the diagonal from the centre down to the east line. Take what lies outside the square, and with a third of it, together with the rest, draw the circle.”
Fig. 1 · The square becomes a circle · cord and pegsstep

Then back, from circle to square. Baudhāyana gives two rules. One is short: cut the diameter into fifteen parts and drop two. The other is one of the strangest sentences in early mathematics:

Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra I.59 · squaring the circle
मण्डलं चतुरश्रं चिकीर्षन्विष्कम्भमष्टौ भागान्कृत्वा भागमेकोनत्रिंशधा विभज्याष्टाविंशतिभागानुद्धरेत् भागस्य च षष्ठमष्टमभागोनम् ॥
maṇḍalaṃ caturaśraṃ cikīrṣan viṣkambham aṣṭau bhāgān kṛtvā bhāgam ekonatriṃśadhā vibhajyāṣṭāviṃśatibhāgān uddharet bhāgasya ca ṣaṣṭham aṣṭamabhāgonam
“Wishing to make a circle into a square, make the diameter eight parts. Divide one part into twenty-nine, and take away twenty-eight of them, and also a sixth of a part, less its own eighth.”

Unpacked, the side of the square is d(1 − 1/8 + 1/(8·29) − 1/(8·29·6) + 1/(8·29·6·8)). It is the same telescoping style as the √2 rule of experiment 01: a whole, then corrections, each a fraction of the one before. Historians have long argued that I.59 was made by running I.58 backwards. The figure below tests that claim directly by running both rules on a real altar, there and back.

Fig. 2 · There and back · does the round trip close?run
TripSquare → circle (I.58)Circle → squareDrift

Run the long rule and the square comes back the same size, to about one part in four hundred thousand. Run the short rule and it shrinks by about 1.4 percent on every trip. The long rule exists to undo the circling rule. Its makers tuned it to the other altar, and that is the property the ritual needed: the household fire and the offering fire can be laid out from each other, in either order, and still match.

Against the true circle, the pair is off. The ratio hidden in both is about 3.088, so the round altar comes out about 1.7% larger than the square it should equal. Nothing in the sūtras names that ratio or checks it against the circumference. The Vedic table asked how to turn one shape into the other and back again, and it answered that question with remarkable care.

02

Table II · Syracusethe problem they could not solve, and the one they solved instead

Archimedes, Κύκλου μέτρησις, Measurement of a Circle · c. 250 BCE

For the Greeks, “square the circle” had a precise meaning: with straightedge and compass alone, in finitely many steps, draw a square equal to a given circle. Hippocrates of Chios squared some curved shapes, his lunes, and raised hopes. Others tried inscribing polygons and claiming that the last one was the circle. Aristophanes was already making jokes about circle-squarers on the Athenian stage. Nobody managed it, because it cannot be done. The proof came only in 1882, when Lindemann showed that π is transcendental.

Archimedes did the next best thing, twice. First he showed what the circle is equal to, in shapes a Greek could handle:

Archimedes, Measurement of a Circle, Prop. 1 · Greek
Πᾶς κύκλος ἴσος ἐστὶ τριγώνῳ ὀρθογωνίῳ, οὗ ἡ μὲν ἐκ τοῦ κέντρου ἴση μιᾷ τῶν περὶ τὴν ὀρθήν, ἡ δὲ περίμετρος τῇ βάσει.
“Every circle is equal to a right-angled triangle in which the radius equals one of the sides about the right angle, and the circumference equals the base.”

The proof is a double trap, the method later called exhaustion. Suppose the circle were bigger than the triangle: inscribe polygons until one is also bigger than the triangle, and reach a contradiction. Suppose it were smaller: circumscribe polygons, and reach another. So it must be equal. This turns the area problem into a length problem. Square the triangle (easy) once you know the circumference. Then he went after the circumference.

Fig. 3 · Circle into triangle · and into Liu Hui's rectangleslide
12

Starting from the hexagon, Archimedes doubled the sides of an inscribed polygon and of a circumscribed one, to 12, 24, 48 and 96. The circle's perimeter is caught between the two. Every step needed a square root by hand, and he gives no account of how he got his remarkably sharp bounds for √3 at the start. He kept every rounding on the safe side, so the trap never leaks.

Fig. 4 · Inside and outside · doubling from the hexagontap
Measurement of a Circle, Prop. 3 · the verdict
τριπλασίων … καὶ ἔτι ὑπερέχει ἐλάσσονι μὲν ἢ ἑβδόμῳ μέρει, μείζονι δὲ ἢ δέκα ἑβδομηκοστομόνοις
“The circumference of any circle is three times the diameter and exceeds it by less than one seventh part, but by more than ten seventy-firsts.” That is, 3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7. In Greek numerals, between σκγʹ/οαʹ (223/71) and κβʹ/ζʹ (22/7).

That is the Greek answer: no square, no single number, but two fractions with a proof that the truth lies between them. Archimedes stopped at 96 sides because the bounds were already good enough to settle the question he cared about, the circle's size to within a hair, proved.

Table III · The counting boarda field to measure, an old ratio to fix, and a knife

九章算术 卷一 方田 · 刘徽注 263 CE · 祖冲之 (429–500), recorded in 《隋书·律历志》

The Nine Chapters meet the circle as a field, 圆田, to be measured for tax and harvest. They give the area four ways, and the first is the deep one:

九章算术 · 卷一 方田 · 圆田术
术曰：半周半径相乘得积步。又术曰：周径相乘，四而一。又术曰：径自相乘，三之，四而一。又术曰：周自相乘，十二而一。
“Procedure: multiply half the circumference by half the diameter to get the area. Another: multiply circumference by diameter, and divide by four. Another: square the diameter, triple it, divide by four. Another: square the circumference, divide by twelve.”

The first rule is Archimedes' Proposition 1, stated as a working procedure: half circumference times radius. The last two quietly assume the old ratio 周三径一, circumference three, diameter one. Liu Hui's first move as commentator is to point out what that ratio really measures. Three diameters is the perimeter of the inscribed hexagon, a shape whose corners touch the circle. The circle itself is longer. To find out how much longer, he starts cutting.

刘徽注 · 圆田术 · 割圆
割之弥细，所失弥少。割之又割，以至于不可割，则与圆周合体而无所失矣。
“Cut it finer and finer, and less and less is lost. Cut and cut again until it can be cut no more, and it becomes one body with the circle, and nothing is lost.”

His only tool is the 勾股 rule from experiment 01. From any polygon's side, half the side is a 勾; the radius is the 弦; the 勾股 rule gives the 股; what is left of the radius beyond it is a small 勾 of a new, smaller triangle, whose 弦 is the side of the polygon with twice as many sides. Repeat. Each pass is one 开方 on the board.

Fig. 5 · 割圆 · from 6 sides to 192 (and on)cut

Liu Hui also had an upper bound, and his was cleverer than it first looks. Each cut adds small triangles, and the difference between two successive polygons, the 差幂, bounds what is still missing. So the circle sits between the 192-gon and the 192-gon plus one more such difference. On a circle of radius one chi (ten cun), that squeezes the area between 314 64/625 and 314 169/625 square cun. He took 3.14, the 徽率 157/50, then pushed on to 3072 sides for 3927/1250, which is 3.1416.

Two centuries later 祖冲之 went much further. His own book of methods, the 缀术, is lost, but the Book of Sui records his result: π lies between 3.1415926 and 3.1415927. Keep cutting Liu Hui's way to 24,576 sides and the squeeze gives exactly those two numbers, so the figure above can reach his result. He gave two fractions for use: the 约率 (rough ratio) 22/7, and the 密率 (close ratio) 355/113, correct to six decimals. Europe found that fraction again only in the sixteenth century.

The maths, side by sideone identity, three uses

Plate V · Śulba · two rules, one hidden ratio
r = s/2 + (√2 s/2 − s/2)/3 = (2 + √2)s/6, implied π = 36/(2 + √2)² ≈ 3.0883
s/d (I.59) = 9785/11136 ≈ 0.878682; s/d (I.58 reversed) = 3/(2+√2) ≈ 0.878680
How to read itLine one is the circling rule as algebra: the radius is the half-side plus a third of the overshoot of the half-diagonal. Turned into a ratio, it says π ≈ 3.088. Lines two and three set the long squaring rule beside the circling rule run backwards. They agree to six places, so I.59 really is I.58 inverted. The 13/15 rule (0.8667) is a quick field version, and it doesn't match.
Plate G · Archimedes · the doubling trap
A = ½Cr; P(2n) = 2pP/(p+P), p(2n) = √(p·P(2n)); 223/71 < π < 22/7
How to read itProposition 1 converts area into length. The doubling step uses p for the inscribed perimeter and P for the circumscribed one: the new outer perimeter is the harmonic mean of the old two, and the new inner one is the geometric mean of the old inner and the new outer. Archimedes did not write it as a formula. He reasoned with angle bisectors and similar triangles, which comes to the same thing. Four doublings from the hexagon give 96 sides and the famous interval.
Plate C · 刘徽 · 勾股, again and again
a = ℓ/2, b = √(r² − a²), c = r − b, ℓ(2n) = √(a² + c²); S(2n) = ½ n ℓ r; S(2n) < S < S(2n) + (S(2n) − S(n))
How to read itLine one is one cut: half a side (勾), the radius (弦), the 股 by root extraction, the leftover small 勾, and the new side. Line two is the trick that saves work: the area of the doubled polygon is half its parent's perimeter times the radius, which is 半周半径 applied to polygons. Line three is the 差幂 squeeze. The circle lies between the polygon and the polygon plus the last gain.
Plate K · why two tables got one number
S(192)/r² = 96·ℓ(96)·r / 2r² = p(96)/d = 3.14103195…
How to read itLiu Hui's 192-gon area, divided by the radius squared, and Archimedes' inscribed 96-gon perimeter, divided by the diameter, are algebraically the same expression. Both stand on half circumference × radius. One table measured fields and the other proved theorems, and both still got 3.14103195.

If the three tables metan imagined exchange · play, built from real lines

Staged, as before. Quoted lines are the traditions' own.

Syracuse

Your round altar is too big, friend. By my bounds the ratio is above 3 10/71. Yours implies 3.088.

Altar

Too big compared with what? Build the square from the circle by our rule and you get the square we began with. Earth and heaven hold the same fire. That is the measure the rite asks for.

Board

The tax-collector asks for a different measure. A field reckoned with 周三径一 is a hexagon, not a field. “割之弥细，所失弥少。”

Syracuse

Then we agree on the method. Polygons inside, polygons outside, and the circle trapped between. At 96 sides I stopped.

Board

At 192 I had your exact number, from areas instead of perimeters. Then I went to 3072. “以至于不可割，则与圆周合体而无所失矣。”

Syracuse

“Until it cannot be cut” is the step I will not take. No polygon is ever the circle. That is why I give you two numbers and a proof.

Altar

And we give you two cords that agree. Everyone here gave two of something.

Here the rigour ends and the wondering begins

Put the two experiments side by side and a pattern holds. With the square, the three tables answered see, make, proceed. With the circle the verbs shift a little, but the characters stay the same.

to balanceAltar · two cords
to boundSyracuse · two fractions
to cutBoard · ever finer

The altar-builders cared about a relation between two shapes that had to stand in for earth and heaven. So their best rule is the one that makes the relation reversible, and the circle's own ratio never needed a name. Archimedes cared about what can be proved. So he gives a window, closed at both ends, and the most honest thing he can say about the number is where it cannot be. Liu Hui cared about a procedure that anyone at a board can continue. So his answer comes with its own refinement built in, and 祖冲之 could pick it up two hundred years later and keep cutting.

The shared ground is just as striking. Half circumference times radius sits under both Syracuse and the Nine Chapters. Both started at the hexagon and doubled. They met at 3.14103195 without ever knowing the other existed. And the fraction the Greek wrote as his upper bound, the Chinese kept as their rough ratio.
