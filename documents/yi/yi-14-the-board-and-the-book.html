易 Yi · 14 · Field note · November 2026 · The board

The boardand the book

Two thousand years of readers who looked at a weiqi board and saw the 易.

Weiqi is played with identical stones on a bare grid. The 易 is read through six lines that are either whole or broken. For most of Chinese history, people who knew both have felt that the two belong together, and they have said so in essays, treatises, anecdotes and, lately, patents. This note follows what they actually said, in order, and asks what kind of connection each of them was making. The next note takes the question into our own hands and bends the board to see what happens.

弈yì
弈 is the oldest name for the game. The Han dictionary 说文 glosses it simply as the surrounding board game, 围棋. In modern Mandarin it happens to sound the same as 易.
What this note does
Sets out the documented associations between weiqi and the 易, marks which are verified and which are reported, and separates three different claims that are usually run together.

00 · Opening

The link is old, sincere, and made by readers.

The short answer to "are weiqi and the 易 related?" is yes, in the way a landscape and a poem about it are related. The game came first. The cosmology was read into it, carefully and with conviction, for two thousand years.

The question arrived in conversation on 14 November 2026, alongside a 2005 Chinese patent for a chess game played on the sixty-four hexagrams. It seemed a small question. It opened onto a long shelf of writing in which a square board and two bowls of stones are asked to carry heaven, earth, the calendar, good government and the pattern of change itself.

Reading that shelf in order shows something a summary hides. The writers are doing different things. One is defending the game's dignity. One is counting. One has a flash of recognition. One is building an archive. One is describing how to play well. Their common ground is a habit of attention: each looks at a field of black and white marks and reads a situation in it. That habit is the real inheritance, and it is the subject of the next note.

A note on evidence. Each item below is marked checked where the text or record was read during this work, and reported where it rests on ChatGPT's research note of the same night or on memory, and still needs to be read against its source before this note is cited by anyone else.
01 · Ground

Two things on a table.

For a reader who has met neither, here is what each one is made of, and why they look like relatives at first sight.

围棋 · weiqi

A grid, two colours, no ranks

Two players take turns placing a stone on any empty crossing of a grid, nineteen lines by nineteen in the modern game. Stones never move. A stone, or a connected group of stones, stays on the board while it touches at least one empty crossing, called a liberty, 气. Surround a group completely and it is lifted off.

Every stone is identical. A stone has no name and no power of its own. What it is worth depends entirely on where it stands and what stands near it.

易 · the Changes

Six places, two kinds of line

A hexagram is a stack of six lines, each whole (yang) or broken (yin), giving sixty-four figures. The book attaches a statement to each figure and to each of its six lines. A reading attends to which lines are present, where in the stack they sit, how they stand to their neighbours, and which of them is about to change.

A line has no meaning alone. Its meaning comes from its place, 位, and its relations.

Set side by side, the family resemblance is plain. Both use two kinds of mark. Both give meaning by position and relation. Both are read for where things are tending. A weiqi player speaks of thick and thin, light and heavy, empty and solid. A reader of the 易 speaks of timeliness, position, the centre, and what is correct. Neither vocabulary names things. Both name conditions.

That resemblance is what every writer below is responding to. What each one builds on it is another matter.

02 · Six readers

Six people looking through the same board.

From the first century to the twentieth. Each entry gives what the writer said, what the writer was doing by saying it, and how far we have checked it.

班固
Ban Gu · 32–92 CE
Eastern Han
checked

The board as government

局必方正，象地则也。道必正直，神明德也。棋有白黑，阴阳分也。骈罗列布，效天文也。

In his short essay 《弈旨》, the historian Ban Gu reads the game part by part. The board is square because it images the earth. The lines are straight because virtue is. The stones are white and black as yin and yang are divided. Spread across the board they imitate the stars. Then he turns: once these four images are set, what happens next lies with the players, and that is kingly government.

What he is doing matters as much as what he says. Confucian opinion was divided on weiqi. It could be condemned as idleness and contention. Ban Gu's essay answers by showing the game to be a model of the moral and political order. Later scholars describe this as the founding move of a whole tradition of writing about the game, in which a skill is shown to reach the Way. The reading gave the game its standing.

敦煌棋经
Dunhuang manual
about the 6th century
reported

The first book of the game

The oldest surviving manual devoted to weiqi was found in the Dunhuang caves and is now in the British Library. It discusses play on a board of nineteen lines. Modern writers quote it as saying that the 361 crossings follow the degrees of the heavenly round, and that black and white are yin and yang. If the quotation is sound, the cosmological reading was already a commonplace by the time anyone wrote a practical book.

张拟
Zhang Ni · about 1050
Northern Song
checked

The board as cosmic arithmetic

局之路，三百六十有一。一者，生数之主，据其极而运四方也。三百六十，以象周天之数。……局方而静，棋圆而动。

The 《棋经十三篇》, the Classic of Weiqi in Thirteen Chapters, opens by counting. There are 361 crossings. One of them, the centre, is the lord from which number is born. The other 360 image the days of the heavenly round. Divide them in four and you have the seasons, ninety to a corner. The seventy-two points of the outer rim image the seventy-two five-day periods of the year. The stones, half white and half black, follow yin and yang.

Then comes the sentence that the next note leans on: the board is square and still, the stones are round and move. A fixed structure, and interventions upon it. The same chapter ends by observing that from antiquity to now no two games have been the same.

Editions differ on whether the author's name is 张拟 or 张靖. The text is modelled on the thirteen chapters of 孙子兵法, and most of it is strategy.

陆九渊
Lu Jiuyuan · 1139–1193
Southern Song
reported

The board as a diagram, suddenly seen

此河图数也。

A story told in the Song miscellany 《鹤林玉露》 has the young philosopher watching games in a market for days, then buying a board, hanging it on his wall and lying beneath it for two more. Suddenly he says: this is the number of the River Chart. He goes back and beats the professional.

Whatever happened, the story records an idea worth keeping. Seeing the pattern is imagined to change what one can do within it. Recognition comes first and skill follows. The 河图 is one of the number diagrams that Song scholars of the 易 placed at the root of the trigrams, so the anecdote is the plainest case of someone claiming to see 易 learning in the board itself.

林应龙
Lin Yinglong · 16th century
Ming
reported

The board as an archive

Lin compiled the twenty-volume 《适情录》. The Qing imperial catalogue is reported to describe its first eight volumes as holding 384 diagrams of play, handed on through the Japanese monk 虚中. The number invites a thought: sixty-four hexagrams times six lines is 384. The catalogue describes a collection of weiqi diagrams and says nothing of the lines of the 易, so the match is a temptation and stays one.

What Lin did is still part of this story. He gathered configurations, sorted them and passed them on. The 易 is also, in one of its aspects, a sorted collection of situations with comments attached. A facsimile of the 《适情录》 was published in Kyoto in 2004, and one of its editors was the last reader on this list.

吴清源
Wu Qingyuan · Go Seigen
1914–2014
checked

The board as one whole

The greatest player of the twentieth century spent his last decades on what he called 六合之棋, the weiqi of the six directions: east, west, north, south, above and below, which in old Chinese is a word for the cosmos. Its aim is 中和, a centred harmony. The best move is the one that brings every stone on the board into balance with every other.

He also held that the game began as an instrument, for astronomy or for divination, before it was a contest, and that people once followed the shifting of yin and yang with white and black stones on a board. That is a claim about origins, and it rests on his authority as a player. His account of how to play is a different kind of statement and a stronger one. It describes whole-board relational judgment, in the vocabulary of the 易, from the inside.

Read together, the six are doing six jobs. Ban Gu dignifies the board. The Dunhuang manual repeats what by then was common knowledge. The Song classic numbers it. Lu Jiuyuan recognises a diagram in it. Lin Yinglong collects its configurations. Wu Qingyuan plays it as a single field. The board is, by turns, an emblem, a calendar, a chart, a library and a practice.

03 · A complication

The board was not always nineteen lines.

The Song arithmetic is beautiful, and it describes a board that earlier players did not use. This is the fact that tells us which came first, the game or the cosmology.

HAN · 17 LINES

289 crossings

The rim holds 64 points. No centre-plus-360. A third-century description gives each side 150 stones.

SUI ONWARD · 19 LINES

361 crossings

The rim holds 72 points. One centre and 360 around it, which is what the Song classic counts.

In the early 1950s a complete stone board was found in a late Eastern Han tomb at 望都 in Hebei. It has seventeen lines each way. That agrees with the earliest full description of the game, by 邯郸淳 in the third century: seventeen lines along and across, 289 crossings, 150 stones for each colour. The earliest board yet found with nineteen lines is a small ceramic model from the tomb of 张盛 at Anyang, dated 595. Historians of the game note that the two sizes probably coexisted for a time, and a seventeen-line board survives in Tibetan play.

So when Ban Gu wrote that the board images the earth and the stones the stars, the board in front of him very likely had 289 crossings. The elegant 360-and-one belongs to a later board. The cosmology was fitted to the game as the game stood in each age.

ChatGPT's research note adds a detail that sharpens this. The rim of the seventeen-line board has exactly sixty-four points, and at least one modern thesis proposes that as the board's original design. The rim of the nineteen-line board has seventy-two, which is the number the Song classic uses. Each board offers a number to whoever goes looking. That is the lesson: on a square grid, meaningful numbers are easy to find, and finding one tells us about the reader.

04 · Sorting the claims

Three different links, usually run together.

When someone says weiqi and the 易 are connected, they may mean any of three things. They stand on very different ground.

Origin
  • The claim: weiqi began as an instrument of astronomy, calendar-keeping or divination, and so descends from the same source as the trigrams.
  • Who makes it: Wu Qingyuan, and a number of modern writers, some of whom derive the board directly from the sixty-four hexagrams.
  • Standing: unproven. The earliest boards and the earliest descriptions show a game. One recent advocate concedes that most readers have taken the old statements as analogy.
Reading
  • The claim: the board and stones can be read as images of heaven, earth, yin and yang, the calendar and the order of things.
  • Who makes it: Ban Gu, the Dunhuang manual, the Song classic, and nearly every preface to a weiqi book since.
  • Standing: fully documented. It is a deliberate practice of interpretation, 立象比德, and it did real work: it made a contested pastime respectable.
Kinship
  • The claim: the two share a structure. Identical marks, meaning by position and relation, attention to tendency, change by single steps.
  • Who makes it: implied by Lu Jiuyuan's recognition and Wu Qingyuan's practice. Stated here.
  • Standing: open, and the only one of the three that can be examined by building something. That is the next note.

Keeping these apart removes most of the confusion. The reading is certain and old. The origin story is attractive and unsupported. The kinship is neither history nor legend. It is a question about how two systems work, and it can be put to a model.

05 · Inventions

When the link is turned into rules.

In our own century the association has been taken literally, by people who built games from it. Two patents show two ways of doing so.

中国易象棋, 2005

This is the document that began the conversation. 陈著全, of 岑溪 in Guangxi, filed a patent application for a chess game of the Changes. It is a relative of chess, and it is the most thorough attempt we found to make the 易 into a board.

The board has sixty-four squares, each carrying one hexagram. They are arranged exactly as the old square diagram of the 先天 order arranges them: rows by lower trigram, columns by upper, running 坤 艮 坎 巽 震 离 兑 乾. Each side has sixteen pieces, named from the cosmogony of the 易 in order of descent: 无极, 太极, 阴 and 阳, the four images, and the eight trigrams. Power follows descent. The earlier a principle, the further its piece may travel. 太极 is the king, and the eight trigrams are the foot soldiers, one step at a time.

下＼上坤艮坎巽震离兑乾坤坤剥比观豫晋萃否艮谦艮蹇渐小过旅咸遁坎师蒙坎涣解未济困讼巽升蛊井巽恒鼎大过姤震复颐屯益震噬嗑随无妄离明夷贲既济家人丰离革同人兑临损节中孚归妹睽兑履乾泰大畜需小畜大壮大有夬乾

The board of the 2005 patent. Rows give the lower trigram, columns the upper. 否 and 泰 are marked: the same two trigrams, 乾 and 坤, in opposite order.

Most of this is naming. One rule goes further. The patent calls it 八卦移位法, displacement by trigram. When two trigram pieces stand side by side, the one that acts becomes the upper trigram and the other the lower. Together they spell a hexagram, and the passive piece is sent to the square that bears that hexagram's name. 乾 acting on 坤 spells 否, so 坤 goes to the 否 square. Had 坤 acted on 乾, the pair would spell 泰 and 乾 would travel to the opposite corner.

否
One real idea

Two pieces, by standing in a relation, compute a place. The board stops being a floor and becomes an index of outcomes. The order of the pair decides the result.

It is a striking mechanism, and the patent's own demonstration game, eighty-six rounds long, is played with that rule switched off. The invention is chess wearing the vocabulary of the 易, with one rule in which the 易 does some work.

A spherical board, 2021

ChatGPT's note reports a second patent, a utility model credited to 刘英男, for a weiqi board on a rotating sphere: twelve pentagons and 150 hexagons, with 320 crossings and three neighbours at every one. We have not read the record. The arithmetic is sound: those faces give 480 edges, and Euler's formula then requires 320 corners.

The two inventions point in opposite directions. The first keeps the flat board and changes what stands on it. The second keeps the stones and changes the ground. The second direction turned out to be the fruitful one for us. Players have explored it for some time: weiqi on a board whose edges are joined into a cylinder or a torus is an established variant, playable online.

06 · Close

What the readers were doing.

Two thousand years of commentary keeps returning to one picture, and the Song classic said it in eight characters.

局方而静
棋圆而动
The board is square and still. The stones are round and move.《棋经十三篇 · 论局》, about 1050

Every reader on this list is working with that pair: a fixed ground, and acts upon it. Ban Gu's earth and stars, the Song calendar and its days, Lu Jiuyuan's chart and the play it unlocked, Wu Qingyuan's whole board and the single stone that must suit it. Square and round are also how the 易 tradition draws its own sixty-four, as a square diagram set inside a circular one.

The readers took the ground as given. None of them asked what the stones would mean if the ground were a different shape. That question needs no new history. It needs a board that can be bent, and a way of reading what the stones say when it is.

The history gives us the pair. The next note takes hold of one half of it and changes it, to see how much of a stone's meaning was the board's all along.
