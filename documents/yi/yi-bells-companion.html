易 Yi · Companion · October 2026 · The instrument behind the listening

The bellsof Zeng

A side quest into the instrument we have been listening through.

Since note 07, every sound in these chapters has been a chime-bell modelled on the set buried with 曾侯乙, Marquis Yi of Zeng, in 433 BCE. The set is more than a sound. Each bell gives two tones. Its inscriptions name twelve pitches by stacking thirds on four roots, and they translate those names between the tuning systems of neighbouring states. Taken together, the set is an instrument, a treatise on tuning and a notation. This companion looks at how it was tuned, how its pitches map onto today's scales, and what kind of diagram its naming makes beside the Western staff and other Chinese notations.

01 · The set

Sixty-five bronzes on three tiers.

Excavated at Suizhou in Hubei in 1978, the set hangs on an L-shaped rack of lacquered wood with bronze fittings. Sixty-four bells hang in eight groups across three tiers, with a sixty-fifth, a 镈 bell, given by King Hui of Chu after the marquis died.

65bells: 19 on the top tier, 33 in the middle, 12 below, plus the 镈 of 楚惠王
C2–D7range, about five octaves; fully chromatic through the middle
3,755inscribed characters, mostly about pitch and tuning
2025inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World register

A schematic of the tiers, not to scale, with the group divisions left out. Strike a tier to hear its register: small 钮钟 above, 甬钟 in the middle and below.

The set's range laid over a modern keyboard. A piano spans A0 to C8; the Zeng bells cover C2 to D7, five octaves and a whole tone. The labelled C is 姑洗, the set's reference pitch, very close to our C.

02 · One bell, two tones

Two places to strike, and a third between them.

Struck at the centre of its face, 正鼓, a bell sounds its a-tone. Struck near the side, 侧鼓, it sounds a b-tone a third higher. Of the 45 middle- and lower-tier bells that were played, 31 sound a minor third and 14 a major third.

The almond-shaped cross-section, 合瓦形, is what makes the two tones possible. A round bell rings on as one blur, but the flattened bronze damps quickly and holds two separate vibration patterns. The minor-third bells also let the set cover all twelve semitones economically. Six bells whose a-tones step by whole tones give one whole-tone scale from their faces and the other from their sides, so together they make the chromatic scale.

The listening chapters arrived at the same structure from the other direction. In note 10, the Yi's harmony held only when yin sat an odd number of semitones from yang, so that the two lived in complementary whole-tone scales. A minor-third bell is that relation cast in bronze: one whole-tone world on the face, the other on the side.

You are hearing synthesised chime-bells modelled on the 曾侯乙编钟, tuned here in equal temperament with 姑洗 on C.
03 · Naming by thirds

Four roots and two kinds of third.

The inscriptions name pitches with four roots, 宫 商 徵 羽, roughly do, re, sol, la. Two suffixes do the rest: -角 adds one major third, and -曾 adds two. Four roots times three forms gives all twelve pitches.

Mi has no root of its own. It is 宫角, a third above do. The suffix seems to have begun as the name of the scale's third degree before a theorist turned it into a general operator, "add a third". Three major thirds make an octave, so stacking a third on 宫曾 brings you back to 宫. The roots themselves form a chain of fifths, 宫 徵 商 羽, C G D A.

Drawn as a grid, the system is a lattice with fifths on one axis and major thirds on the other. Leonhard Euler drew that same lattice in 1739 and called it the Tonnetz, and nineteenth-century theorists made it a standard tool of Western harmony. Because thirds wrap after three steps and fifths after twelve, the lattice closes on itself into a torus, the same kind of surface that carried the sixty-four in note 06. 04 · Many states, one pitch

The inscriptions translate between tuning cultures.

Beside each pitch name, many inscriptions give what that pitch is called in other systems: Zeng's own, Chu's, Zhou's and those of other states. The bells are a dictionary between musical languages.

Zeng used six standard pitch names spaced a whole tone apart. Chu used twelve, a semitone apart, and named six of them by flattening the others with the prefix 浊, "muddy". The same sound could carry a different name in each state. Where Zeng said 姑洗, Jin said 六墉. The lower-tier inscriptions go further and give each note several definitions at once, one for each key it might belong to, the way a modern musician knows that E is the 1 of E, the 2 of D and the 3 of C.

The Zeng standards are six whole-tone names, the same structure that note 10 heard in 乾, the 六律. The difference between whole-tone and semitone systems was a live question between neighbouring courts in the fifth century BCE, and the marquis had both written onto his bells.
05 · Notation as diagram

Five ways to write one phrase.

The same five notes, the rising pentatonic 宫 商 角 徵 羽, C D E G A, written five ways. Each notation is a diagram that keeps something different.

Europe · c. 1025

The staff

Axes: time across, pitch up

Guido of Arezzo's lines make music a graph. Every note has coordinates: when it sounds, and how high. It records the sound itself.

China · Tang onward

工尺谱

上尺工六五
One column of named degrees

Characters name scale degrees, read top to bottom, right to left. It records which step of the scale, and leaves the key movable.

China · late Tang

减字谱

One composite glyph per action

The qin's tablature, credited to Cao Rou, builds each sign from fragments: which string, which stud, which finger, which stroke. It records the hand, not the pitch, and leaves the rhythm to the player. The glyph here is a schematic of how a sign is built, not a real character.

Rousseau 1742 · China 1900s

简谱

1 2 3 5 6
Numbered degrees, movable do

Numbers stand for degrees of whatever key is chosen. Proposed in France, it became China's everyday notation in the twentieth century.

Zeng · 433 BCE

钟铭 · bell names

宫 商 宫角 徵 羽
A lattice of relations

No melody is written down. Each note is placed by its relation to the others, a fifth here and a third there. It records where a pitch sits in the web, and how other states name the same place.

Put side by side, each diagram answers a different question. The staff asks where in time and pitch. The 工尺 and numbered notations ask which step of the scale. 减字谱 asks what the hand does. The Zeng inscriptions ask what each pitch is to the others, and to the neighbours. The last is the closest to how these chapters have been listening: a hexagram heard as a relation among voices more than as a list of notes.

06 · Still wondering

Threads left open, on purpose.

Wondering

Two tori

The Zeng naming closes into a torus of fifths and thirds; the sixty-four close into a torus of lower and upper trigrams. Is there a map between the two surfaces, or only a family resemblance?

Wondering

Thirds before fifths?

Chinese tuning theory is usually told as a chain of fifths, 三分损益. The bells' names are built from thirds. Practice in bronze and theory on bamboo may have spoken different dialects.

Wondering

A dictionary of tunings

The inscriptions treat different tunings as translations of each other. That is close to this archive's working principle of holding tunings lightly.

Hold lightly

The synthesis

The bells heard here are an imitation built from published measurements, tuned in equal temperament. The real set is tuned by ear in bronze, within a few percent of its targets, and sounds like itself.
