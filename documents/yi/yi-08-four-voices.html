易 Yi · 08 · Field note · October 2026 · The listening, continued

Four voicesof a line

and a first song, sung loosely.

A line of the Yi is named by a number, 九 or 六, and cast as one of four: six, seven, eight or nine. Heard as music, the two names stand a fifth apart, and the four values become a voice that is either at rest or leaning toward its turn. This note follows those voices into the tradition's own tuning, lets the milfoil cast a phrase, and plays the rising and falling tide of the twelve sovereign hexagrams as a first song — in whatever tuning the listener prefers.

00 · Held lightly

The tuning is an instrument, chosen for now.

The previous note let its fields sing on a pentatonic scale. That was one choice of instrument. The song of change asks for no particular scale, and the chapters from here offer several tunings side by side, privileging none.

The same melody can be sung by a qin, a choir or a set of bells and remain itself; what holds it together is the movement between its notes, the arrangement and the timing. The instrument here is the chime-bell set of 曾侯乙, and its own way of sounding is the default: each line is one bell, yang struck at the face and yin at the side, a minor third higher. The tunings in section 04 are lenses on the same movement. The bells' major third, the Han pipe tuning in which yin sits a fifth from yang, and a free slider sit beside the default, so the listener can hear what changes and what persists.

The instrument chosen for these chapters is the chime-bell, after the set buried with 曾侯乙, Marquis Yi of Zeng, in 433 BCE: sixty-five bronzes spanning some five octaves, made to be struck singly or together. Each of its bells sounds two tones — one when struck at the centre of its face, 正鼓, and another, a major or minor third higher, when struck at the side, 侧鼓. One bell, two tones: the pair is already there in the bronze. Here each line of a hexagram is a bell, and yang and yin are two places to strike it.

Strike a bell at its centre (正, yang) or its side (侧, yin). The pitches follow the tuning chosen in section 04.
01 · Nine and six

Yang and yin, a fifth apart.

Yang lines are called 九 and yin lines 六. Nine to six is three to two — the ratio of the perfect fifth, the interval from which the whole Chinese pitch system was generated.

The Han treatise on pitch and calendar sets the ground pipe 黄钟 at nine 寸 and 林钟, the pipe a fifth away, at six, and reads the pair 九六 as the way of yin and yang. The twelve pipes are generated by 三分损益: take two thirds of a length, then four thirds, and so on around the cycle of fifths. Zheng Xuan's 爻辰 then assigns the six lines of 乾 to the six yang pipes and the six lines of 坤 to the six yin pipes.

Laid out in order of generation, the assignment folds neatly. Each line position takes two consecutive steps of the cycle: its yang pitch, then the yin pitch generated directly from it. Every line's yin is its yang's fifth.

LineYang · 乾Yin · 坤Yin ÷ yang

One more thing appears in the folding. In the lower trigram each yin pitch sits a fifth above its yang; in the upper trigram each sits a fourth below — the same pitch class, sounded from the other side of the octave. The inner and outer trigrams voice their consonance in opposite directions.

The bells you hear by default voice yin a minor third above yang, as the Zeng bells do; choose the Han pipe tuning in section 04 to hear the fifth that the names 九 and 六 imply. This reading also sits beside the octave reading of the previous note, rather than replacing it. Heard as octaves, the lines are binary and the sixty-four Walsh fields are their harmonics. Heard as fifths, yin and yang are two different pitches that belong together. The Yi seems to hold both: the arithmetic of 2, and the music of 3.
02 · The milfoil's tuning

Four values, unevenly drawn.

The cast gives each line one of four numbers. Seven and eight are young lines, at rest. Nine and six are old lines, about to turn into their opposite. The yarrow-stalk procedure does not draw them evenly.

Yarrow stalks · 蓍草揲法
Three coins · 金钱卦
1 in 4chance any line is moving, by either method
17.8%casts with no moving line at all
35.6%casts with exactly one moving line

Both methods give yang and yin equally often and the same share of moving lines. They differ inside the turning. Under the coins, old yang and old yin are equally likely. Under the stalks, an old yang line appears three times as often as an old yin, while a young yin line is the most common draw of all. Yang tends to arrive already leaning toward its turn; yin tends to arrive at rest. The randomness the tradition used was itself tuned, and the older method carried the asymmetry.

03 · A cast as a phrase

A turning line is a suspended note.

Each line becomes a voice with four states: resting yang, resting yin, and the two old lines, which sound their pitch while already leaning toward the other. The 本卦 is the chord as cast. The moving bells are then struck again on their opposite tones, and the chord they arrive at is the 之卦.

You are hearing chime-bells modelled on the 曾侯乙编钟 (Marquis Yi of Zeng, 433 BCE), struck singly or as a group.

A bell cannot slide, so a turning line is struck again on its other tone, as a 编钟 player moves from one striking point to the next: the turn is heard as a second strike, then an arrival. Where no line moves, the chord simply holds. In the 左传 records, this is the form every cast took — X 之 Y, a chord and the chord it leans into.

The cast is a pseudo-random draw with the method's own probabilities. The phrase plays in the tuning chosen in section 04.

04 · The first song

The tide of the twelve, sung loosely.

The twelve 消息卦 rise from 复 to 乾 and fall from 姤 to 坤, one line turning at each step. It is the tradition's own bounded song: twelve chords, eleven modulations, and a return.

Two hearings are offered, after the two readings of a hexagram in the previous note. Chord strikes the six bells together and strikes the turning bell again on its new tone as the month changes. Melody sings each hexagram from 初 to 上, one bar at a time. Choose a tuning, or set the distance between yin and yang yourself and hear where the song still holds.

Tuning 1.500
Hearing
You are hearing chime-bells modelled on the 曾侯乙编钟 (Marquis Yi of Zeng, 433 BCE), struck singly or as a group.

Each row is a line and a bell, 初 at the bottom; each column a month of the tide. Filled cells are struck at the face, 正, for yang; open cells at the side, 侧, for yin. With the Han pipe tuning the cells show pipe names instead. The thread marks the bell that turns into the next chord. Bells 四 to 上 hang an octave higher, keeping the upper trigram above the lower — a voicing choice, like every other here.

05 · Still wondering

Threads left open, on purpose.

This chapter answers nothing it was asked. It listens further, and leaves these hanging until the chapters come to rest and can be looked at together.

Wondering

A line of four voices

If each line has four states, a cast lives in a space of 4⁶ = 4,096 chords, of which the sixty-four hexagrams are the resting ones. What shape is that larger space, and where in it does a song like the tide run?

Wondering

Octave and fifth together

The fields of note 07 hear in twos; the pipes hear in threes. A tuning that holds both — doubling for structure, the fifth for consonance — might be what 九六 already implies.

Wondering

The lean before the turn

The milfoil makes yang lean more often than yin. Is that asymmetry heard anywhere else — in the text, in the sequence, in the tide's own rising and falling?
