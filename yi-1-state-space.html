The I Ching is three thousand years old and describes itself as a book about change. Almost everything built on it since has been static — a fixed set of sixty-four names you draw from like cards.

This is an attempt to give it back its motion. Not by proving the old cosmology with modern maths, and not by flattening it into generic systems theory. By taking its central move seriously: read the configuration, attend to the transformation, situate the reading.

Before we startWhat is broken

A book about change, held perfectly still.

Strip away the mysticism and the I Ching contains a piece of engineering. Six positions, each one of two values, giving sixty-four configurations — and a body of commentary about how those configurations turn into one another. As a notation for the state of a situation, it is remarkably economical.

What it lacks is everything between the states. A line is yin or yang and nothing else — no sense of barely yang, no sense of a line that has been yang for years and one that flipped this morning. The tradition knows this gap exists; that is what 变爻, the changing line, is for. But the notation cannot hold it, so the knowledge went into the commentary instead, as story.

So the question this sequence asks is narrow and technical: what happens if we let the notation hold it? Give each line a magnitude and a velocity. Then see what the old structure looks like from inside a model that can actually move.

Three things went missing when the lines became bits: how strongly, how fast, and which way.

Four short chapters. This one builds the space — and by the end of it, the two cubes you may have already seen in the instrument will look like the only sensible way to draw the thing.

Chapter oneOne line

A 爻 yao line is a dial, not a switch.

Start with the smallest object in the system. A single line: unbroken is 阳, broken is 阴. One bit of information.

Now replace the bit with a number between −1 and +1. The drawn line is just the sign of that number — so nothing classical is lost, and two new things are gained: how far the value sits from zero, and how close it is to flipping.

Plate 01 · the sign map
x_i in (-1, +1) → sign(x_i) = solid if x_i >= 0, broken if x_i < 0
How to read itEverything to the left of the arrow is what the model keeps. Everything to the right is what the classical glyph draws. The arrow is where the information goes missing — and it is one-way.
阳 solid line阴 broken linexi the stored value

That single change already buys something the tradition wanted and could not write down. A 变爻 stops being a special mark and becomes an ordinary fact about a number: it is near zero, and it is moving. No separate category needed.

Chapter twoThree lines

Three dials make a cube.

Trigrams stack three lines, so we now have three numbers. And three numbers that each run from −1 to +1 are a point in a cube. This is not a metaphor or a visualisation choice — it is what three numbers are.

Watch the space get built one axis at a time.

Here is the part worth slowing down for, because it is where most attempts to draw this go wrong.

A trigram is not a corner of the cube. It is a whole eighth of it.

The eight corners are the extreme cases — every line at full strength. But a trigram is defined by the signs of the three numbers, and an enormous number of different points share the same three signs. Two situations can be "the same trigram" and be nowhere near each other.

Plate 02 · the counting survives
2^1 = 2 2^2 = 4 2^3 = 8 2^6 = 64
How to read itOne line has two states, three lines have eight, six lines have sixty-four. These are the classical counts exactly. Making the lines continuous adds room inside each region without changing how many regions there are.
2ⁿ regions for n lines8 = 八卦64 = 六十四卦

This is the first real gain. In the classical notation, "we are in 巽" is the whole of what can be said. In the cube it is the beginning: where in 巽, how close to which wall, and — once we add motion in the next chapter — heading towards which one.

Chapter threeSix lines

Why there are two cubes.

A hexagram is six lines, so the state is six numbers, so the space is a six-dimensional cube with 2^6 = 64 regions. The sixty-four are still there, exactly. The problem is only that nobody can draw a six-dimensional cube.

There are two ways to respond to that. The tempting one is to squash six dimensions into three and accept the distortion. The better one is to notice that a 6-cube factors perfectly into two 3-cubes — Q6 = Q3 × Q3 — and that the split the mathematics wants is a split the tradition has been using for three thousand years.

Lines 1–3 are the lower trigram. Lines 4–6 are the upper. Two cubes. Nothing lost.

A point in the left cube together with a point in the right cube specifies one point in six dimensions exactly. No projection, no distortion, nothing thrown away. And the region counting works out on its own: eight regions on the left times eight on the right is sixty-four.

Plate 03 · why two cubes
Q6 = Q3 (lower x1x2x3) × Q3 (upper x4x5x6) 8 × 8 = 64
How to read itA six-dimensional cube is the product of two three-dimensional ones, and the factorisation the mathematics wants is the one the tradition has always used. This is not a compromise for the sake of drawing; it is an identity.
Q₃ a trigram cube下 lower · 上 upper× the product, not a projection

Why not nest the cubes instead?

An earlier version of this work drew one big cube with eight small cubes hung on its eight corners — the lower trigram as the outer cube, the upper as a little cube attached at each corner. It looks clever and it is wrong, for the reason Chapter Two spent so long on: a trigram is a region, not a corner. Hanging the upper cube on a vertex attaches it to the extreme case rather than to the eighth of space it is supposed to sit over.

Two plain cubes side by side make a weaker-looking picture and a true one. That trade is worth making every time.

✓

What we now have

Six continuous numbers. Sixty-four sign-regions, intact. A drawing with no distortion in it. And room, inside every region, for situations the classical glyph cannot distinguish.

→

What is still missing

All of the motion. Nothing here moves yet — this is still a snapshot, just a much richer one. Chapter Two of the sequence gives the six numbers velocities, and that is where 变爻 gets a clock attached to it.

Two honest projections beat one clever wrong one.

Part two
Making it move

Give each of the six numbers a velocity. 变爻 stops being a mark and becomes a time: which line crosses next, and in how long. Then the idea that the six lines run at six different speeds — sluggish at the bottom, twitchy at the top — which turns the hexagram's stacking order into something with real work to do.

Part three
What moves it

五行 as five operators rather than five substances — a vector field over the whole space. The 生 and 克 cycles as a coupling that makes the mix circulate rather than settle. And 后天 as a moving target the state is pulled towards, which is where “the situation” finally enters the mathematics.

Part four
The instrument, and what it found

The live simulation, plus the results that came out of it: how long a reading stays valid, which of the sixty-four a situation can actually reach, and the finding that surprised us most — that the operator which rescues you under constraint actively harms you without it.

Each part stands alone and each one ends with something you can push on. If you want the destination first, the instrument is Part Four; it will make considerably more sense with these two cubes already in hand.
