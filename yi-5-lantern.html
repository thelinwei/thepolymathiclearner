The Petrie mandala

Projecting the six-cube onto its Coxeter plane gives the famous twelve-fold rosette — a single flat figure, architecturally the most natural thing here, essentially a plan. But fifteen states collide: only forty-nine distinct points come out, and the minimum separation is exactly zero. As a plan it is a lie in the same way the nested cube was.

Normalising onto a sphere

The obvious cure for the zonotope's hidden interior: keep the direction, discard the radius, and every state lands on the surface. It halves the model. Thirty-two directions for sixty-four states — the radius was the information, and throwing it away merges each state with another.

TwoThe survivor

Height is the yang count. The curve is the change.

Two ideas survived the tests, and they fit together so exactly that they are better read as one.

The first is a height. Count the yang lines in a hexagram and you get a number from zero to six. There is exactly one hexagram with none — 坤 — and exactly one with all six — 乾. In between the counts are six, fifteen, twenty, fifteen, six. Use that count as elevation and the widths give a silhouette on their own: a point at the bottom, a point at the top, widest in the middle.

The second is a thread. There exists a closed ordering of all sixty-four hexagrams in which every consecutive pair differs in exactly one line — a Hamiltonian cycle on the six-cube, which is what a Gray code is. Verified: sixty-four beads, every step a single 变爻, and the sequence closes back on itself.

Now put them together. Because each step of the thread turns one line, the yang count changes by exactly one at every step — so the thread cannot help but rise and fall through the levels, and it never stalls. Measured: the longest run at any single height is one. It is always moving.

Every body chooses what to make visible.

The lantern is one object, holds all sixty-four distinctly, hides nothing inside, and is not a grid. It also gives up something real, and the pattern across four iterations is now clear enough to state as a rule.

There may be no single body that shows everything.

Six dimensions do not fit in three, and each honest projection chooses a different three things to keep. The two-cube view keeps the trigram structure and loses the unity. The zonotope keeps the symmetry and loses half the states. The lantern keeps the unity, the change and the yang-count and loses the trigrams. That is not a failure of any of them; it is what projection is.

Which suggests the actual answer is not one body but one body and one plan — the architect's own convention. Elevation and plan are not two objects; they are two drawings of one. The lantern is the elevation: it shows change and level. The 四象 cube, flattened, is the plan: it shows the trigram pairing. Neither pretends to be the whole, and together they do not read as a pair of rival solids — which was the thing you objected to.
