織 zhīThe loom — the sixty-four on a torus, and what the thread turned out to be

The lantern held everything except the trigrams. The torus fixes exactly that, and does it without adding a second body — because a torus has two circulations, and the lower and upper trigrams are each a closed ring of eight.

The thread was expected to become a knot. It does not. What it becomes is a shuttle: the lower trigram advances once around and never reverses while the upper runs back and forth across it, seven passes to every advance. The torus is not a doughnut. It is a loom.

See the model first ↓
I

Why a torus, and not just a nicer cylinder.

The lantern's single failure was specific: it made the yang count, the change and the poles legible, and it hid the trigram structure completely. There was nowhere in it to see that lines one to three form a lower figure and four to six an upper one — and so nowhere to see the pairing between a line and its partner three places up.

A torus has exactly two independent circulations, a major and a minor. If each trigram can be laid out as a genuine closed ring, then the two trigrams become the two directions of one surface — and the split that forced two cubes becomes two ways of going around a single body.

The question is whether eight trigrams can be arranged in a ring where each step turns one line. They can. It is the same Gray-code argument as the lantern, one dimension down.

How to read itMajor angle from the lower trigram, minor angle from the upper. Verified bijective: sixty-four cells, sixty-four hexagrams, nothing doubled and nothing hidden — every state sits on the surface where you can see it.

And the adjacency behaves. Each trigram has three neighbours in the cube, and on the ring they land at offsets ±1 and one chord at ±3 — so a hexagram's six neighbours are four adjacent cells and two chords, three in each direction. Nothing is lost; two of the six are simply drawn as short arcs across the surface rather than as steps to the next cell.

How to read itVerified for all sixty-four hexagrams with zero exceptions: three neighbours along each circulation. The chords are three steps around, not diameters, so they stay local — they read as a weave rather than as wires across a void.
II

A knot was expected. That was wrong.

The lantern's thread — the closed circuit through all sixty-four where every step turns one line — can be drawn on this surface. When the torus was proposed the thread was said to become a knot, and its knot type to be a property of the ordering rather than of the drawing. That was a nice sentence and it is false.

Computed: the thread winds once around the major circle and zero times around the minor. Homotopy class (1, 0). It is an unknot — a loop that can be shrunk on the surface without cutting. No knot, no invariant, nothing to classify.

But the reason it comes out that way is the interesting part, and it was not visible until the numbers came back.

Plate 4 · what the thread actually does
warp: 8 advances of θ, never reversing · weft: 56 passes of φ, 28 up + 28 down · (p,q) = (1,0)
How to read itOf the sixty-four steps, eight move the lower trigram and fifty-six move the upper. The eight lower advances are evenly spaced — one every eight steps — and every one of them goes forward. The lower trigram never backtracks. The upper accounts for all the reversing: twenty-eight forward, twenty-eight back, netting zero, which is why the minor winding is zero.

The lower trigram is the warp. The upper trigram is the weft. The Gray ordering is the weaving pattern.

Eight advances against fifty-six passes: a ratio of one to seven. The warp is drawn taut around the major circle once and never reverses, and the shuttle runs across it seven times for each advance. That is not a metaphor imported to make the form sound nicer — it is what the step counts are, and it is why the winding class is (1,0) rather than a knot. A knot would require the thread to wander in both directions. A weave requires precisely that it does not.

an unexpected corroboration

Counting the steps also showed how often each individual line turns over one full circuit. The answer is a doubling cascade: 2, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 from the bottom line to the top.

Which is the conjecture from Part Two — that the six positions run at six different speeds, sluggish at the base and twitchy at the top — arriving here unbidden, from a completely different direction. There it was asserted and fitted to nothing. Here it falls out of the Gray ordering as a matter of arithmetic. The bottom line turns twice per circuit; the top line turns thirty-two times.

That was not built in — but the ordering that produces it was chosen, and Section IV shows that the choice is doing all the work. Read this box as a description of the reflected Gray code, not as evidence for the conjecture.

III

The form.

One body, sixty-four cells on its surface, the two trigrams as its two directions, the thread as a single closed curve running warp and weft. Tap any cell to see its hexagram and its six neighbours.

IV

The surface is settled. The ordering is the choice.

With the torus fixed, one thing is still free: the order you thread the sixty-four beads in. That looked like a detail. It is the whole remaining design space, and four things came out of testing it — two of which correct claims made earlier on this page.

One · the chords are forced

Enumerated exhaustively: the cube graph has exactly six Hamiltonian cycles up to rotation and reflection, and every one of them puts the two chords at ±3. There is no kinder ring to be found. The weave must have those two diagonal stitches per cell.

Two · the frequency cascade is this project's, not the book's

The cascade 2, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 was presented as the timescale conjecture arriving unbidden. It arrives because the reflected Gray code was chosen, and a reflected Gray code is defined by that doubling. Across 2,197 sampled orderings the spread in turn counts ran 12 to 26, median 18 — and not one was as skewed as the reflected code's 30. It sits at the extreme edge of the space.

So the corroboration was circular. The hierarchy is a property of the ordering, and there are orderings where all six lines turn at nearly equal rates. Use the balanced button above and watch the warp disappear.

Three · the thread cannot knot

A knotted ordering was said to exist, and a (2,3) trefoil named. Wrong, because the condition that matters had not been checked: a curve only has a knot type if it does not cross itself. That trefoil crosses itself five times, so it has no knot type at all.

And the pattern is much stronger than one counterexample. Only about 4% of orderings are embedded — crossing-free — at all. Among 144 embedded ones, not a single one knots: every winding class has min(|p|, |q|) ≤ 1, including cases like (3, 1) and (−4, 1) where one winding grows large and the other never does.

Conjecture: an embedded thread through all sixty-four cannot knot on this torus.

One hundred and forty-four out of one hundred and forty-four is suggestive, not a proof, and the proof is still wanted. But the knot idea is dead as an aspiration — which is a relief, because it was the last piece of this hunt wanted for its own sake rather than because the mathematics offered it.

Four · and the reflected code earns its place differently than expected

The genuine virtue of the reflected Gray code is not its skew. It is that it has zero self-crossings — it lies flat on the surface and never passes over itself, which puts it in that rare 4%. This had never been checked and never claimed; it is the best property the loom has, and it turned up by accident while trying to disprove something else.

There was also a brief belief in a trade-off — that a clean weave cost you an uneven one, since the first balanced ordering found crossed itself nine times. That was one sample mistaken for a frontier. Mapping it properly turns up an embedded ordering with the optimal spread of 2: turns 10, 10, 12, 10, 12, 10, no crossings, warp and weft exactly 32 each. Balance and cleanliness are compatible, so the reflected code's extremity buys nothing that evenness could not also have.

The balanced one is a plain weave — no warp, no shuttle, no privileged direction, winding twice one way and once the other. Same surface, same sixty-four cells, same six neighbours each. Only the thread differs.

The ordering looked like a detail. It is the only thing left that is a decision.

What it gives up relative to the lantern is the silhouette. In the lantern the yang count was elevation, so 坤 and 乾 were the poles of the form and balance was its equator — you could read the whole distribution from the outline. On the torus the yang count is not a direction at all; it becomes a colouring, and the profile says nothing. That is a real loss and it is why colour by yang is in the controls rather than being the default.

So the pair actually worth keeping is not lantern-or-loom. It is 灯 for the distribution, 織 for the structure — and unlike every earlier pair, these two are not rival solids competing to be the object. They are one surface and one section through it.

A knot would need the thread to wander both ways. A weave needs precisely that it does not.

What to test next

織

Is the chord placement forced?

The two chords land at ±3 because of the particular Gray ring chosen for the eight trigrams. There are other Hamiltonian cycles on the cube graph. Whether any of them puts the chords somewhere kinder — or whether ±3 is forced for all of them — is a small, finite, checkable question, and the answer decides how clean the weave can be.

爻

Does the cascade survive a different ring?

The 2, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 cascade came out of the standard reflected Gray code. A different Hamiltonian circuit through the sixty-four would give a different frequency profile — and if some circuit gives a cleaner geometric progression, that circuit is a better candidate for the timescale conjecture than the one Part Two assumed.
